How it works, and what to trust

Three papers, three jobs

The reading is built in layers, coarse to fine, so the sturdiest measurement does the most work.

Arousal — Sisode (2016), India. That paper proposes the beta-to-alpha ratio as an indicator of how stirred up you are, and that is what drives the arousal figure. It is a whole-band ratio, so it holds up well on one dry electrode.

Valence — found in Kao's tables. Comparing the positive and negative member of each of Kao's pairs, total beta is higher for the positive one by 1.18 and 1.04 percentage points. That is the strongest separator anywhere in the three papers, and Kao's own method does not use it.

Identity — Kao, Wang & Chang (2015), Taiwan. 15 people, acoustic stimuli, an energy table per emotion. Each 1 Hz slice is marked 1 if it holds over 4% of the total, giving a 22-digit signature per emotion. This picks the final answer from the field that arousal and valence have already narrowed.

Framework — Li, Chao & Zhang (2019), Taiwan. Their survey supplies the band definitions used throughout, and the valence-arousal model that the two axes above sit in. Their strongest-performing feature, differential entropy, is the log of band power — which is what the baseline comparison here computes.

Where the two papers appear to disagree

Sisode found alpha higher in happy than sad. Kao's tables show alpha lower in the positive member of both pairs. They are measuring different things: Kao's pairs are both high-arousal contrasts, while happy versus sad also differs in arousal. Alpha follows arousal; beta follows valence. Reading them that way, the two results agree.

What it cannot do

Only 6 of the 22 digits differ between the eight emotions, and each sits within about half a percentage point of the 4% line. Joyful puts 3.62% in the 11–12 Hz slice; Angry puts 4.13%. One dry forehead electrode and a 10-bit converter cannot reliably tell those apart — which is exactly why arousal and valence now narrow the field first.

Surprised and Fear share one signature. The paper says so, and separates them by which delta slice is strongest. That is built in, but it relies on the 0.5–1 Hz range, where electrode drift lives, so it is the shakiest link.

A note on gamma

Li's survey lists gamma under happiness, because that paper describes every band in emotional terms. The broader literature ties gamma to attention, conscious perception and working memory, which is what most charts say. This dashboard uses the wider description. Nothing downstream changes: Kao's emotion data stops at 22 Hz, so gamma never enters the emotion decision. It also sits above the BioAmp EXG Pill's roll-off near 44 Hz, and on a forehead electrode it picks up jaw and eyebrow muscle as readily as brain activity.

Which reading to trust

They are not equally reliable, and they answer different questions. The mental state asks how your brain is running; the emotion asks which of eight feelings you are having. If the two ever disagree, believe the mental state.

Mental state is built on whole-band power, where the differences are several standard deviations wide. Testing it on simulated recordings: eyes closed scored 7.8, concentrating 11.1, drowsy 6.7 — all far past the 2.4 threshold needed to report anything at all.

Emotion rests on six digits that sit within half a percentage point of the 4% line. Under realistic noise its accuracy falls from near-perfect to roughly half. It is a faithful implementation of a published method, at the edge of what this hardware can resolve.

Getting a clean reading

Keep your eyes open and fixed on one spot. Closing your eyes raises alpha far more than any emotion does.
Sit still during the baseline. The first 30 seconds learn what your resting brain looks like.
Press the electrodes down if the signal bar turns red.
